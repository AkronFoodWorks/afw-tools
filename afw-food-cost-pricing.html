Ingredients

Enter each one as you bought it. Bulk cases are handled for you.
Serving several dishes? List the ingredients for all of them here and tag each with its dish (Lasagna, Gumbo…). The tool totals the food and divides by your guest count. Don't track every gram — get the big-cost items right.
Trim / yield % is how much of what you buy is actually usable after peeling, trimming, or loss — 100% for flour, maybe 70% for whole produce or bone-in meat. Enter the usable amount in the recipe field above, and the trim % quietly adds back the cost of what you threw away. That hidden cost is where most pricing goes wrong.
4

Packaging & disposables

Cost of what each single unit ships in.
Disposables belong here — foil pans, deli containers, paper, bags, labels — they're part of every unit's cost. Durable smallwares (sheet pans, a mixer, a slicer) are one-time business purchases, not per-unit costs — leave those out for now. Sorting and tracking those is a separate tool coming later.
5

Your time & any help

Pay yourself — even if you reinvest it.
$
If a client pays $750, the part you keep is what's left after your own time is paid for. Putting your hours in here is what turns "what's in my pocket today" into a real margin you can plan on. Reinvesting the difference is then your choice — not a hidden subsidy.

Kitchen time

Your booked hours at AFW, spread across the batch.
$
Food trucks: AFW asks for a minimum of 8 commissary hours a month. If a batch uses less, you'll still pay toward that floor — worth remembering when you set volume.
7

Event extras & fees

Rental costs vs. fees you bill the client — kept separate.
These are costs. The tool spreads them across your guests, so they count against your margin.
$
Separate from the extras above. A delivery or service fee is revenue you add on top of the per-guest price. It shows in the client's quote total but stays out of the per-guest margin, so it won't distort your food pricing.
